## Structure 5: The Case Study Deep Dive

### What It Is

A single real-world example — a person, channel, video, or strategy — is dissected to extract transferable principles. The example does the credibility work so the creator doesn't have to assert expertise; the evidence speaks. The structure has two modes: (A) first-person (creator analyzes their own results) and (B) third-person (creator analyzes someone else). Both use the same skeleton: establish the result → deconstruct the mechanism → extract the principle.

### Beat-by-Beat Flow

1. Hook: Establish the extraordinary result (specific number, outcome, person) — this is what makes people click
2. Click-confirm: Show the proof (play the video, show the screenshot, state the metric) — earn the right to teach
3. Origin/context: How did this happen? Where did it start? (establishes stakes + story)
4. Mechanism analysis: What specifically caused the result? Break it into components (idea quality, hook structure, editing choices, distribution)
5. Live tools/demo: Often includes playing the actual video, showing analytics, or demonstrating tools used
6. Extracted principles: 3-5 transferable lessons stated explicitly ("the reason this worked was...")
7. Outro: Recap extracted framework → CTA to apply it

### Videos Using This Structure (7)

- How I Got 46 Million Views From ONE Short-Form Video (self-analysis: idea + script + edit)
- How Steven Bartlett Built a Content Machine That Makes $25M/Year (third-party: 5-step framework + echo effect)
- Social Media Expert Breaks Down $100M Content Strategy (third-party: Wander channel audit)
- What I Learned After 1 Year Of Being A Full-Time Creator (self: metrics + wins/losses + learnings + strategy)
- Do THIS To Blow Up Your Brand in 2025 (SuperUGC Strategy) (case study: tabs, NZT examples → framework)
- Copy This Hook, It'll Blow Up Your Social Media (framework built from case studies: 5 templates × examples)
- I Studied 100 Viral Hooks, These 6 Will Make You Go Viral (research-driven: 6 formats from studied examples)

### When to Use

When you have a compelling real-world result that earns the right to teach. Best for topics where showing is more powerful than telling (growth, monetization, viral content). Third-person case studies let you borrow credibility from well-known creators.

### Variations

- **Self-autopsy variant:** Creator dissects their own viral/successful content (46M views, year-1 retrospective). Highest authenticity signal.
- **Third-party breakdown variant:** Analyzes an external creator/brand (Steven Bartlett, Wander channel). Can attach to large names for search/interest.
- **Research-synthesis variant:** Studies many examples (100 viral hooks, 10,000 videos) to extract patterns — hybrid of investigator and case study. The "research" is the credential.

### Signature Moves (Advanced Techniques)

- **The "diamond in the rough" framing.** When analyzing other creators' content, use the phrase "a great idea executed poorly" to position the work as a refinement, not a copy. This reframe earns the right to critique without sounding arrogant.
- **The four-bucket assessment.** In the 1,000-hours video, assess every outlier across four buckets (idea/premise, hook structure, story structure, visual structure). This is a repeatable evaluation framework that works on any piece of content.
- **Playing the source material.** In case study videos, show the original video or the analytics before analyzing it. This "see it before I explain it" move is a trust device — it prevents claims from feeling invented.

#### Case study analysis — Toyota City four-bucket assessment
**Original (source: It took me 1,000+ hours to learn this about content):**
> So I was scanning for AI video ideas one day and I came across my friend Rowan's channel. I saw this video that was about Toyota building a secret tech city in the foothills of Mount Fuji. And his video didn't have crazy views — it was only like a 2x outlier at the time. But for whatever reason, I clicked on it. And so I watched that video. And this was my base assessment across these four buckets. First, the idea and premise. The idea and premise of this was extremely strong. Essentially, Toyota's building a super smart city to test all the latest AI and robotics. So there will be people living in the city and they'll have the latest and greatest AI automation, smart homes, and they're really using it as like a future city test bed buried secretly at the foothills of Mount Fuji. That's a banger idea. And as I searched it more on YouTube to research it, I found tons of great visuals from Toyota illustrating what the city could look like. So immediately I knew this idea would be interesting to my IVA — which are tech enthusiasts. So on the idea front, with the premise, I didn't really have to change anything. The premise would be to explain that this city exists and why it's so interesting. But in this case, the reason I identified this video as an opportunity for me is that it was a great idea executed poorly. That's what you're looking for. All right. Next we move on to the hook structure. So when I watched Rowan's hook delivery in this video, I saw a bunch of things that could be immediately improved. For one, the hook was delivered with super slow pace and low energy. The edit led with full screen B-roll that had a big logo of Toyota, which felt more like a commercial than an interesting video. One of the sentences he used right away was a non-important fact that 100 people in the city were going to move in soon — he pulled that fact too high up. He also didn't create enough contrast as to why this city was more interesting than another city. But I saw that the raw visuals he used in the hook had a ton of potential. So for all these reasons, I knew right away the hook could be improved. Tighter, clearer, more contrast, better pacing. Now the third thing is story structure. And this video was built around what's called a newscaster structure, which begins with a hook but then lists a bunch of facts over and over. Newscaster is pretty good, but I knew if I swapped this to what's called a breakdown structure, I'd have a lot more success. Breakdown structures use facts but they rehook intentionally throughout, which is why you can hold the viewer for a bit longer. Essentially his version got boring too fast because of a lack of embedded contrast. Now I love Rowan. His version of this video got 40,000 views. My version of this video got 2.2 [million].

**Template:**
> So I was [SWAP: scanning for / researching / studying] [SWAP: domain] one day and I came across [SWAP: the specific example]. And [SWAP: their result] was [SWAP: not spectacular but interesting]. But for whatever reason, I [SWAP: clicked/engaged/investigated]. And this was my base assessment across these four buckets. First, the [SWAP: idea / premise / concept]. [SWAP: Assess: was it strong or weak, and why.] But in this case, the reason I identified this [SWAP: example] as an opportunity for me is that it was a [SWAP: great idea executed poorly / strong concept with weak execution]. That's what you're looking for. Next, the [SWAP: hook / opening / packaging]. [SWAP: Assess what was wrong and what had potential.] I knew right away the [SWAP: hook/opening] could be improved. [SWAP: Specific improvements: tighter, clearer, more contrast]. Now the third thing is [SWAP: story structure / format / organization]. [SWAP: What structure they used and why you'd change it.] And lastly, [SWAP: visual structure / execution]. [SWAP: Assess and compare results.] Their version got [SWAP: original result]. My version got [SWAP: improved result].

**What makes it work:** The four-bucket framework (idea, hook, story structure, visual structure) is a complete evaluation template that any creator can use to assess an outlier video. "A great idea executed poorly — that's what you're looking for" is the most actionable line in the entire corpus for content remixers.
