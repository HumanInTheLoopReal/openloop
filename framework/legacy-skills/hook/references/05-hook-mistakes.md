# Hook Mistakes: Complete Guide

> The four reasons viewers churn in the first 5-15 seconds — named, defined, and fixable.

## Overview

Kallaway identifies exactly four hook mistakes. Every viewer churn in the hook phase is caused by exactly one of these. They form a decision tree: each mistake is a gate the viewer passes or fails in sequence. Fix them in order — Delay first, then Confusion, then Irrelevance, then Disinterest.

**The 4 mistakes in sequence:**
1. **Delay** — Topic context comes too late
2. **Confusion** — Words/phrasing are unclear
3. **Irrelevance** — Viewer doesn't believe it's for them
4. **Disinterest** — Hook doesn't create curiosity

**Diagnostic logic:**
- Did they know what the video was about fast enough? → Delay
- Did they understand the words? → Confusion
- Did they believe it was for them? → Irrelevance
- Did they feel compelled to know more? → Disinterest

**Source concentration:** All 4 named mistakes come from a single primary transcript (*Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*). Secondary sources confirm and extend each mistake's definition.

**Cross-references:**
- Delay = opposite of Speed to Value (see 04-hook-psychology)
- Disinterest = solved by creating Contrast and Curiosity Loop (see 04-hook-psychology)
- Irrelevance = solved by Ontarget Curiosity (see 04-hook-psychology)
- Confusion = solved by Topic Clarity (see 04-hook-psychology)

## Mistake 1: Delay

**What it is:** The context on what the video is going to be about arrives too late. Easiest mistake to fix.

**How to identify it:** Visualize your script. Which line is the FIRST line that introduces the topic? If it is not line 1, you have delay. Everything above that line is fluff.

**Fix:** Cut all lines above the topic introduction. Move that line to the top.

**Teaching layers:**

Named definition + fix:
> the first hook mistake is called delay. And the good news is this one is the easiest one to fix. When I say delay, what I mean is that the context on what the video is going to be about is delayed too long in the video. If you were to visualize a script, let's say it has 13 lines and the third line is the first time you introduce the topic. This is delay because those first two lines are fluff and unnecessary details. You need to cut those first two lines, move the third line up to the top so your topic introduction can happen in the first 1 to 2 seconds

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

Classic bad example (the "craziest thing" opener):
> Guys, this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen. And when you see it, you're never going to believe it. That is an example of a legit hook that many people try to use when they're beginners. Now, in theory, this kind of sounds like it might be a good hook because it's building suspense and curiosity about what that thing is. You might hear that and wonder, "What did the creator see? I have to stick around to find out

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

**Why the "craziest thing" opener fails:** Zero context on what is coming. The viewer cannot assess whether they care. They may stay for one line out of shallow curiosity, but the hook has not earned their attention — it has borrowed it.

Secondary — value race framing:
> demonstrate value or incoming value as soon as possible. Content is basically a race to see if you can signal value before they lose interest

— Source: *How to Write a Script So Good Viewers Can't Stop Watching*

**What he does but doesn't teach:** Kallaway's own hooks never have preamble. The topic is sentence 1. "Today we're talking about hooks" — that is already the topic, but in practice his hooks are even more compressed: "I studied 100 viral hooks" names the subject and the action in 5 words.

**Fix template:**

Before (with delay):
> Guys, I've been thinking about this for a while and I wanted to share something with you. So I was doing some research this week and I came across something that blew my mind...

After (delay removed):
> [SWAP: topic directly]. [SWAP: why it's interesting].

## Mistake 2: Confusion

**What it is:** The viewer understands a hook is happening but cannot process what you are saying. Comprehension loss. The words used or sentence structure makes meaning ambiguous. Different from Delay — topic is named, but the words are unclear.

**Fix:** Simplify vocabulary, reorder for clarity, never assume embedded context. Front-load the conclusion, not the setup.

**Teaching layers:**

Named definition:
> hook problem and mistake number two is confusion. And when I say confusion, what I really mean is comprehension loss. So, in this case, the viewer is just not understanding or comprehending what you're saying. At this point, you've eliminated the delay, which is good, but the words you're using or the phrasing of those sentences is just not clear and it's confusing the viewer. So, the problem here is really just a lack of clarity in your words. And the reason this is a problem is again, if the viewer can't assess if this video is actually for them because they're only understanding like half the words you say, then they have trouble deciding whether or not to opt in

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

Before/after rewrite — journaling example:
> 30 million empire and the online money they made is most difficult to earn if you don't develop a journaling practice like they did. And this is a bit exaggerated obviously. Few people actually talk like that, but you get the point of how difficult and confusing it was to actually parse through what I was trying to say. If you hear just that, you can kind of gather the video is going to be about an online business and maybe something to do with journaling, but you're really struggling to put the pieces together clearly because of the way I framed the sentence. And this is a classic clarity issue. Instead, for that exact same idea, you could write the hook like this. These guys built a $30 million empire and their secret for earning money online was their insane journaling practice. See how just changing the words slightly and the way the sentence is ordered makes it way easier to understand what I'm saying

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

**The journaling rewrite dissected:**

Before: "The online money they made is most difficult to earn if you don't develop a journaling practice like they did."
- Passive construction
- The cause-effect order is reversed (conclusion after condition)
- "Most difficult to earn" is ambiguous
- "Like they did" requires knowing what they did

After: "Their secret for earning money online was their insane journaling practice."
- Active construction
- Subject → action → revelation (linear, front-loaded)
- Every word is simple and unambiguous
- Same information, dramatically clearer

**What he does but doesn't teach:** The pattern in the fix is always subject → verb → revelation, not condition → result. Never "if you do X, you get Y." Always "X produces Y" or "The secret is Y." Front-load the conclusion; the viewer's brain processes it faster.

**Fix template:**

Before (confusion pattern):
> [SWAP: outcome/revelation] is [SWAP: hard/complicated] if you don't [SWAP: method/condition] like [SWAP: the character/who did it] did.

After (clarity pattern):
> [SWAP: The subject/character]'s secret for [SWAP: achieving outcome] was [SWAP: the revelation/method].

## Mistake 3: Irrelevance

**What it is:** The viewer knows what the video is about and understands the words, but does not believe it applies to their specific situation. They do not see themselves in the creator's framing. Two root causes: (a) does not see themselves in the creator, (b) does not see the incoming value applying to their problem.

**Fix:** Reframe the hook using "you" and "your" language. One-word shift from "I" to "If you" — massive impact.

**Teaching layers:**

Named definition:
> Problem number three, irrelevance means that the viewer is not confident that what's coming next in the video after the hook is going to be valuable enough to help them solve a problem

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

"I've struggled" → "If you've struggled" reframe:
> I've struggled with skin problems my whole life. The problem is this opens a door in the viewer's mind to question if they see themselves in the creator because they're thinking this, well, if you struggle with these problems, do I also struggle with the same problems you do? That's what they're questioning in their mind. If the viewer doesn't see themselves in the creator for a number of different reasons, they're going to put less value in the incoming solution, and they'll stop watching. This is the irrelevance problem. The viewer doesn't feel like what's coming next will be relevant for them. So, to solve this problem, all you have to do is frame your hooks using you and your. So instead of saying, "I've struggled with skin problems my whole life," you say, "If you've struggled with skin problems your whole life," dot dot dot. When you do this, the viewer feels like the video is targeted for them

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

Secondary — signal why they should care:
> viewer does not understand why the video matters to them. And this can happen for two reasons. Either the viewer didn't think the video was relevant to them, or they just weren't interested in the topic at all. Now, if they weren't interested in the topic, no worries. Your hook would never have hooked them because they weren't going to stick. But if they would have been interested and the hook didn't work, the reason it didn't work is because you didn't signal why they should care and stay to watch

— Source: *Give Me 18 Minutes, and I'll Improve Your Video by 193%*

**The key distinction:** If the viewer was never going to be interested in the topic (wrong audience), the hook could never fix that. Irrelevance is the mistake when the viewer WOULD have been interested but was not hooked — because the framing did not signal "this is for you."

**What he does but doesn't teach:** Kallaway's own hooks almost never use "I've done X" as the opening. He uses "I" for credentialing (in the body, not the hook), but opens with "you" framing or impersonal facts. The first hook line is rarely about him; it is about the viewer's situation or the world.

**Fix template:**

Before (Irrelevance risk):
> I've [SWAP: done / struggled with / figured out X] my whole [SWAP: life / career / year].

After (Irrelevance fixed):
> If you've [SWAP: struggled with / wanted to / tried to X your whole life], [SWAP: this changes everything / here's what actually works].

## Mistake 4: Disinterest

**What it is:** The viewer has passed the first three tests — they know the topic, understand the words, believe it's for them — but the hook still doesn't grab them. No curiosity loop is opened. This is the hardest mistake to fix because it is a storytelling problem, not a mechanical one.

**Fix:** Create contrast. A (current belief) vs B (your contrarian alternative). The distance between A and B is what triggers curiosity. B alone reactivates the pain of A not solving their problem.

**Teaching layers:**

Named definition + curiosity loop + contrast mechanism:
> number four is disinterest. And this is the one that most people fixate on the most. At this point, the viewer is clear on the topic, believes there is some relevance or value incoming for them, but the hook still just doesn't grab them enough. This is the curiosity problem. The hook just doesn't make the viewer curious enough to want to keep watching. So, what you want to do in the hook to solve the disinterest problem is build a curiosity loop. A curiosity loop is when the viewer sees something, asks a hypothetical question in their mind, gets some additional context to answer it, but that spurs a new question, more context, new question, more context, and so on. So, your job in the hook is to open that initial curiosity loop and start the spiral... The way you do this is by setting up contrast or comparison in the hook. Contrast is simply the distance between the current common belief of the viewer and some contrarian or alternative perspective that you offer.

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

Acne example (contrast in action):
> this one trick will clear up all your acne in 45 minutes without any side effects. What I'm really doing is creating a comparison in your mind to the current baseline. You got to wait 6 months, take Accutane, go through all these side effects versus my contrarian offer, 45 minutes, no side effects, quick solve. That distance is contrast.

— Source: *Give Me 15 Mins, and I'll Make Your Hooks Impossible to Skip*

Secondary — relative comparison framing:
> hook never gets the viewer curious enough to want to stay. And the exact mistake you're making is that you're not introducing the new topic in a relative way compared to something they already understand. For example, if I say this new plasma is hotter than asphalt, that's not really going to hook you. Why not? Because you don't really know what hotter than asphalt means... But if I say this new plasma is hotter than the surface of the sun, you do know what the surface of the sun should be. And so that makes you more hooked... Always introduce new things as a relative comparison to something that exists. And what this really is is contrast. And that's the root of where all curiosity comes from

— Source: *Give Me 18 Minutes, and I'll Improve Your Video by 193%*

**What he does but doesn't teach:** Disinterest is the only mistake Kallaway does not give a simple mechanical fix. The acne example is illustrative but requires deep understanding of contrast mechanics. The real skill is identifying what the viewer's A belief is (their current baseline understanding), then crafting B to be as far from it as possible while remaining believable.

**The contrast formula:**

A = the viewer's current baseline (Accutane, months, side effects)
B = your alternative (45 minutes, no side effects)
Contrast = the gap between them

The bigger the gap, the stronger the curiosity loop. The viewer's subconscious reactivates the pain of A when hearing B — because B implies A has been unnecessarily making them suffer.

## Checklist

- [ ] **Delay check:** Is the topic named in the first line? If not, find the first line that names it and cut everything above it.
- [ ] **Delay check:** Does the hook avoid "craziest thing I've ever seen" type openers with no context?
- [ ] **Confusion check:** Read the hook aloud. Does every sentence parse on first listen?
- [ ] **Confusion check:** Is the sentence structure subject → verb → revelation (front-loaded conclusion)?
- [ ] **Confusion check:** Any jargon or terms the viewer must already know to understand?
- [ ] **Irrelevance check:** Does the hook use "you" and "your" framing?
- [ ] **Irrelevance check:** If using "I", can the viewer easily project themselves into the "I"?
- [ ] **Irrelevance check:** Does the hook signal why this is for the target viewer specifically?
- [ ] **Disinterest check:** Does the hook create a contrast between A (current belief) and B (your alternative)?
- [ ] **Disinterest check:** Is B introduced as a relative comparison to something the viewer already understands?
- [ ] **Disinterest check:** After reading the hook, does the viewer have an involuntary question they need answered?
