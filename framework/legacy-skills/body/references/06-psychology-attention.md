# Psychology of Attention: Complete Guide

> How Kallaway uses viewer psychology — checkpoints, dopamine mechanics, curiosity loops, and comprehension — to hold attention through the entire body of a video.

## Overview

This guide covers how Kallaway thinks about the viewer's brain while writing the body. Every body technique he teaches has a psychological mechanism underneath it — open loops, curiosity amplitude, dopamine release, comprehension loss. This guide names those mechanisms and shows how to apply them directly.

The body's primary psychological mission is Checkpoint 5: getting the viewer to pay attention. Checkpoints 1-3 happen in the hook and intro. Checkpoint 4 (likability) builds through the body. Checkpoint 5 is what all reh-hooking, loop management, and comprehension work is actually doing. Checkpoint 6 (action) is the outro's job.

**How to use it:** Read this guide before writing a body outline, not while editing. These are the mental models that shape which points to include, in what order, and how to frame each one. The template bank at the end gives copy-paste lines for the moments where you need to trigger curiosity, open a loop, or reset attention.

## Six Viewer Checkpoints (Gate Sequence)

### What It Is

Kallaway boils down every video into six steps. He treats them like gates or checkpoints — the viewer must pass through each one before the video can succeed. He uses a combination of different psychology hacks to pull them through each gate.

The six checkpoints in order:
1. **Pain acceptance** — viewer accepts the pain point you've described
2. **Trust** — viewer trusts you enough to keep listening
3. **Plan of attack** — viewer sees a clear plan for what you're about to deliver
4. **Likability** — viewer likes you as a person
5. **Paying attention** — viewer is actively present throughout the body
6. **Taking action** — viewer acts on the CTA in the outro

### How to Apply

1. Map your script to the six checkpoints. Identify where each gate is opened and closed.
2. Checkpoints 1-3 are the intro's job. If any of the first three are not cleared by the end of your intro, the body fails before it starts.
3. Checkpoint 4 (likability) builds across the body — primarily through solving real problems and showing your genuine passion for the topic.
4. **Checkpoint 5 is the body's primary job.** Every reh-hook, open loop, and comprehension repair technique is in service of this gate. The body is the checkpoint 5 machine.
5. Checkpoint 6 is the outro's job — but the body has to build enough trust and value so the viewer cares about the CTA.

### When to Use

Every single video. The six-checkpoint frame is not a format — it's a viewer psychology model that applies regardless of structure. Use it as a diagnostic when a video isn't holding retention: which checkpoint did you fail to clear?

### What He Does But Doesn't Teach

He doesn't map the checkpoints to script timestamps explicitly, but the architecture of his videos does it implicitly: pain in the first 5 seconds, trust credentials within 15 seconds, plan within 30 seconds, first likability signal via humor or empathy at 45-60 seconds, then the body sustains checkpoint 5 from minute 1 onward.

## Brain as Problem-Solving Machine

### What It Is

Human brains are problem-solving machines. If you give a brain a problem, it is designed to figure out how to solve it. When you start a video script with the problem, you are hacking the viewer's neurochemistry to get them to pay attention. This first step is critical because it aligns their subconscious on the problem so that you can tell them the rest of the story.

Before watching the video, the viewer may not have been problem-aware. Or if they knew the problem, they weren't consciously focusing on it. Framing the problem explicitly angles their attention — and once it's angled, the brain wants to stay until the problem is solved.

### How to Apply

1. Open your video with the problem, not the solution. The brain will not engage with a solution to a problem it hasn't accepted yet.
2. Make the problem specific enough that the viewer recognizes it as theirs. Vague problems produce vague engagement.
3. Every open loop in the body is a sub-problem. The brain must close each one. This is why curiosity loops work — they are micro-problems that compel sustained attention.
4. Do not rush to the solution. Let the brain sit with the problem long enough to feel the itch before offering the scratch.

### When to Use

All educational content. The principle is especially powerful in pain-first hooks and reh-hooks that re-agitate the problem mid-body. Any time attention is decaying, naming a new problem resets engagement.

## Dopamine Formula (Value + Contrast + Relevance)

### What It Is

Dopamine release when watching a video comes from three different factors: value, contrast, and relevance.

- **Value** is what you're actually getting out of the video — the utility or purpose.
- **Contrast** is the distance between what the viewer expected and what you actually delivered.
- **Relevance** is how targeted the content is at the specific viewer.

Together: value + contrast + relevance = maximum dopamine, and therefore maximum addictiveness.

### How to Apply

1. For every body point, score it on two or three of the three factors. A point that hits only one rarely hooks.
2. **Value alone is not enough.** Pure information without surprise (contrast) or personal relevance is forgettable.
3. To maximize contrast, anchor to the common belief first, then subvert it. The surprise is the contrast.
4. To maximize relevance, narrow your audience. The more specifically your point is aimed at one type of viewer, the higher the dopamine because it feels tailor-made.
5. If a body point feels flat during editing, check which of the three factors it's missing.

### When to Use

Use this formula as a point design filter during scripting. Apply it when auditing a draft body: does each point have value? Does it have surprise? Does it feel personally relevant to your target viewer?

## Value Density (Distillation, Not Editing Speed)

### What It Is

Value density is how packed with entertainment or usefulness a video is. The common misconception is that value density means faster editing or more cuts. That is wrong.

What value density actually means is simplifying the story, cutting out all unnecessary details, and increasing clarity of the script. If you have seven valuable things to say, instead of saying it in 14 minutes, could you cut it down to eight? Shorter is always better. Shorter is more distilled.

### How to Apply

1. For every sentence in your body, ask: does this have to be here? Does it make the viewer want to read the next line?
2. Cut anything that exists to demonstrate your expertise rather than transfer it. Your credential is in the insights, not the word count.
3. Compare the original draft to a compressed version at 60% length. If the compressed version loses no essential meaning, the original was not dense enough.
4. Never pad to fill a length target. A 4-minute video with maximum value density outperforms a 12-minute video at average density.

### When to Use

At the editing stage of any script. This is the pass that happens after a complete draft — not while generating. In long-form especially, value density separates watchable from forgettable.

### What He Does But Doesn't Teach

He says "shorter is always better" but his own long-form videos are 15-20 minutes. The resolution: his long videos are dense because he's teaching entire systems per video. Length is appropriate when the density stays high throughout. The rule is not "be short" — it's "be dense."

## Comprehension Maxing (Visual Matching)

### What It Is

Comprehension maxing is what Kallaway calls the golden goose of storytelling psychology. Comprehension is how well a viewer actually understands what you're saying in a video.

When someone starts watching, their understanding is at 100%. But every sentence, every graphic, every additional word you say has the potential to drive that comprehension down. When a viewer gets confused — comprehension loss — they stop watching because nobody likes being confused.

The easiest way to increase comprehension is visual matching: pairing every sentence you speak with a visual that precisely represents what you're saying.

### How to Apply

1. After writing the script, go line by line and ask: what visual exactly matches this sentence?
2. If a sentence has no clear visual match, rewrite the sentence until it does — or cut it.
3. Avoid visuals that require separate explanation. If the viewer has to decode the graphic, comprehension drops.
4. Use B-roll, screen recordings, diagrams, and text overlays to reinforce the spoken word — not illustrate different points simultaneously.
5. Simpler sentences = easier visual matching. This is another reason atomic messaging reduces comprehension loss.

### When to Use

All visual content — primarily YouTube. The principle is less directly applicable to audio-only content, but the underlying logic (reduce cognitive load per sentence) applies to all scripting.

## Curiosity Loop Mechanics

### What It Is

Curiosity loops work because the brain releases massive amounts of dopamine the moment a viewer is captivated by an open question they ask in their own head. The loop is the open question. The closure is the answer.

The bigger and more non-obvious the question, the bigger the curiosity, the higher the stakes, and the more dopamine. The more relevant the question to the viewer's pain, the bigger the curiosity. Both dimensions — size and relevance — are required for maximum impact.

### How to Apply

1. Before opening a loop, establish relevance. The viewer has to care about the answer before the question creates pull.
2. Open the loop with a question that is non-obvious. An expected question creates no tension. A question where the viewer doesn't know the answer — and didn't anticipate the question — creates maximum pull.
3. Do not let loops stay open too long without dopamine rewards. In long-form, reopen smaller loops mid-way to keep the brain engaged while the larger loop closes.
4. Close every loop you open. Unclosed loops destroy trust.

### When to Use

Every body point can open a micro-loop before the teaching. This is the standard teaching rhythm: pose the question (or imply it), then answer it. In reh-hooking, you open a new loop right before transitioning to the next point.

## Curiosity Amplitude

### What It Is

Curiosity amplitude measures the size of that curiosity — specifically, how badly the viewer wants the answer to the open question. The closer the question is to a pain point the viewer is actively experiencing, the longer they will wait for the answer.

If you get a question to pop in a viewer's brain but it's trivial or surface level, their attention will be extremely fragile. A high-amplitude curiosity question is close to something they're currently struggling with or afraid of.

### How to Apply

1. For each body section's open loop, ask: is the question connected to a pain point the viewer actively has? If not, they won't wait.
2. Use the viewer's actual pain language in the question. Their word for their frustration, not your technical term for it.
3. Curiosity amplitude determines how long you can hold a loop open before closing it. High amplitude = more patience. Low amplitude = close it quickly.
4. When building long-form videos, you can sustain longer loops only if you've confirmed the pain connection in the hook.

### When to Use

Use as a calibration check when planning the body's open loops. High-amplitude loops carry the video. Low-amplitude loops are filler.

## Dopamine Ladder (6 Levels)

### What It Is

There are six stages of a viewer's journey when watching content that release increasing amounts of dopamine. Ascending a viewer through all six stages means they watched the entire video and want to come back for more.

1. **Stimulation** — The first 1-2 seconds. Visual stun gun. Colors, motion, brightness. Stops the scroll.
2. **Captivation** — The curiosity stage. Open question pops. Viewer enters a loop to hunt for the answer.
3. **Anticipation** — The question grows. The viewer believes the payoff is coming and starts anticipating it.
4. **Validation** — The loop closes with a non-obvious answer. Reward lands. More dopamine releases.
5. **Affection** — The viewer likes you. This is the likability checkpoint. Solved problems build it fastest. See `08-psychology-trust.md`.
6. **Revelation (Pavlovian)** — Consistent delivery of levels 1-5 eventually creates a conditioned response: dopamine releases just from seeing your face or name. See `07-psychology-emotion.md`.

### How to Apply

1. **Stimulation:** The first 2 seconds of the video must have visual contrast. Unique color, motion, or brightness. This is the thumbnail-to-video bridge.
2. **Captivation:** The hook must open a specific, non-obvious question the viewer cares about.
3. **Anticipation:** Build a plan section in the intro. When the viewer knows what's coming, they anticipate each reveal. The ordered list of body points IS the anticipation architecture.
4. **Validation:** Every body point must close with a non-obvious insight. Expected answers fail at validation. Aim for the answer that reframes their understanding, not confirms it.
5. **Affection:** Solve real problems. The likability shortcut is genuine utility.
6. **Revelation:** This is the result of 100+ reps. You cannot engineer it in a single video.

### When to Use

Use as a video-level audit tool. After scripting a video, check: does the video build through all six stages? Where does it break the ascent?

## Bottoms-Up Processing (Subconscious Visual Recognition)

### What It Is

Under the hood of viewer neurochemistry, the brain processes visual content in two stages. The first stage is rapid subconscious processing — called bottoms-up processing. This happens in approximately 200 milliseconds. At this speed, the eye and brain are catching vivid colors and motion only. They are not comprehending what they're seeing.

This is the mechanism that determines whether a video gets a pause on the scroll or not. Bottoms-up processing must be won before any content is processed.

### How to Apply

1. The first visual frame of your video must win the bottoms-up test. Unique color, brightness, or motion — before any content is comprehended.
2. This is why thumbnails and opening frames matter disproportionately. Not because viewers consciously evaluate them, but because they are processed subconsciously in 200ms.
3. Do not rely on the value of your topic to stop a scroll. The brain hasn't processed the topic yet. Win stimulation first.

### When to Use

Thumbnail design, opening frame selection, short-form video design. Any format where the viewer is scrolling past the video before deciding to watch.

## Shock Value / Interestingness (Distance from Known)

### What It Is

Interestingness comes from shock value. When you're making a video, whether entertainment or education-based, you want to find key facts or opinions that drive maximum shock. Shock value is the distance between what someone already knows about a topic and what you tell them in the video. The greater this distance, the greater the shock and the more they will hold on to see why that shocking claim might be true.

Shock value is quantifiable: Kallaway uses a Shock Score of 1-100. Out of 100 people, how many would have heard this fact or opinion before? If four out of five people wouldn't have heard it, that's a shock score of around 80. The goal is to find facts that are true but also have a maximum shock score.

### How to Apply

1. While researching, score every fact and insight on a 1-100 shock scale: out of 100 people in your target audience, how many already know this?
2. Build your body around your highest-scoring facts. 80+ shock score = body anchor material.
3. Never lead with low shock-score facts. They set a low expectation pattern. Reserve the most shocking facts for body points 2 and 3.
4. Check that every high-shock fact is also true. Shock without accuracy destroys trust. The combination of surprising and verifiable is the sweet spot.

### When to Use

Use at the research phase, before scripting. This is a point-selection tool, not a writing tool. Apply it to identify which of your research findings deserve body real estate.

## Checklist

- [ ] Identify which of the six checkpoints each section of your script addresses
- [ ] Confirm checkpoints 1-3 are cleared within the first 30 seconds (intro)
- [ ] Design the body to sustain Checkpoint 5 (paying attention) throughout
- [ ] Open the first 2 seconds with visual stimulation — color, motion, or brightness
- [ ] Frame the first body point as a problem the brain wants to solve
- [ ] Score each fact and insight with a 1-100 Shock Score during research; build body around 80+ scores
- [ ] Check every body point against the dopamine formula: does it have value + contrast or relevance?
- [ ] Identify the visual match for every line in the script; if no clear match, cut or rewrite the line
- [ ] Open a curiosity loop at the start of every body point; confirm each loop is tied to a real viewer pain
- [ ] Calibrate loop duration to curiosity amplitude — only hold loops open as long as the pain connection supports it
- [ ] Check that every opened loop is closed
- [ ] Audit for value density: is there anything in the body that could be cut without losing meaning?
- [ ] Confirm at least two non-obvious insights that deliver Validation (Dopamine Ladder Level 4)
