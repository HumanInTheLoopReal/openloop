# Idea Development

## Overview

Idea development is the research and validation phase that happens before scripting. It sits between selecting a topic and writing the outline. The goal is to find the most interesting angle on a topic, validate it against what is already working in your niche, and assemble the seven components of a complete video idea before you write a single word of script.

Without this phase, you are guessing across all seven dimensions of your video simultaneously. With it, you use data to lock in the components that are already proven and only invent where you have a genuine edge.

This phase produces: (1) a list of high-shock facts about the topic, (2) a validated angle and hook format, (3) a chosen story structure, and (4) a clear desired emotional outcome — everything needed to start the outline.

## Concepts

### 1. Idea Legos

**Definition:**

> "A video idea is made up of seven different components. I call these idea Legos. You have the topic, but you also have the angle, the hook, the story structure, the visual format, the key visuals, and the audio. Think of these seven different components as Lego bricks that assemble together to create a unique video idea."
>
> "The topic is the one sentence simple description of what the video is about. But the angle is the combination of the premise, take, and interesting key facts that are used to explain the topic. So, you could have two branding videos about Apple. One of them about the history of their logo design and the other one about a specific ad campaign from 2004. These are very different videos. Similar topic, completely different angles."
>
> "The hook structure is made up of three parts. The spoken hook, what is said. The text hook, the text that is shown on screen, and the visual hook, what is shown and how it's shown. The story structure is the way the story is delivered. Could be a breakdown, a listical, a tutorial. Think of it like story blocks or an outline structure that determines how the story flows. The visual format is the way the video is laid out visually. Could be green screen, could be a POV vlog, could be a faceless music only text conversation. There are dozens and dozens of winning visual formats."
>
> "The key visuals are the actual visuals used in the layout. It's what's actually shown on screen. The A-roll, the B-roll, the animations, etc. The visual format is how the visuals are laid out and sequenced. The key visuals are the visuals themselves. And then lastly, you have audio, which is either the background music or sound effects."

— Source: the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

**How to apply:**

1. When evaluating an idea, break it into all seven bricks: topic, angle, hook format, story structure, visual format, key visuals, audio.
2. Do not treat topic as the full idea. A topic without the other six bricks is not a complete idea.
3. When analyzing outlier videos, label each of the seven bricks explicitly. This is the raw material for remixing.
4. When your video underperforms, diagnose which brick failed — not "the video didn't work."

**When to use:** Every time you develop a video idea, whether from scratch or from an outlier. The Lego framework is the atomic unit of all idea work.

**Source:** the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

### 2. Outlier Research

**Definition:**

> "The first step in the process is to find other videos in your niche that have already performed well. We call these outliers and we measure them using an outlier score. An outlier score takes the views of one video divided by the average views of that channel. So, for example, if some video gets 100,000 views and the average views on that channel is around 10,000, that would be an outlier score of 10x."
>
> "Outlier scoring is really important because it helps us compare different sized channels apples to apples. Small channels typically don't get as many views on average. But if they have a banger video with a 50x outlier, then that signals something different happened there that's worth studying."
>
> "If I showed you a hundred videos and said, 'I'll give you a million dollars if you can make one that beats all 100.' What would you do? Well, the first thing is you would rank the hundred from best to worst. And you would study the best ones. You look for gaps, identify where they were weak, and then try to make your version better than the weak points of the best videos."

— Source: the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

**How to apply:**

1. Build a watch list of 20–30 top channels in your niche.
2. Filter videos by outlier score 5x and above, engagement rate 5% and above (to screen out paid placements), and a relevant post date range (7 days for news-based, 3–12 months for evergreen).
3. Sort by outlier score descending.
4. For each outlier, watch the video manually first, then review the transcript and hook/story structure analysis.
5. Log findings in a Google Doc: topic, angle, hook observations, key visuals, audio notes, and gaps you noticed.

**When to use:** At the start of every idea development session, before determining your angle or hook. The outlier database tells you what the market has already validated.

**Source:** the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

### 3. Law of Interesting

**Definition:**

> "Interestingness comes from shock value. When you're making a video, whether it's entertainment or education based, you want to find key facts or opinions that drive maximum shock. Now, shock value is the distance between what someone already knows about a topic and what you tell them in the video. The greater this distance, the greater the shock and the more they will hold on to see why that shocking claim might be true."
>
> "For example, I made a video about a Russian engineer that built a fully functional Iron Man suit in his garage. Now, when I was researching this topic, I came across a clip where the main engineer said that he invented the world's first personal hydrogen reactor, the glowing thing that goes in Iron Man's chest. As soon as I heard that, I thought, there's absolutely no way that could be true. How could a random guy in a garage build a hydrogen reactor by himself? And that right there, that curiosity shocked based question that pops in your head when you hear something you can't believe, that's what you're going for. My current belief was that there was a 0% chance this guy could have built a hydrogen reactor. His claim was that he did. The distance between those two things is massive. And that distance represents max shock, max interestingness."

— Source: how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

**How to apply:**

1. When researching a topic, watch and read everything you can find.
2. For every fact you encounter, estimate the distance between what the average viewer already believes and what this fact claims.
3. The larger the distance, the higher the interestingness.
4. Look specifically for the moment when you personally think "there's no way that's true" — that reaction in you is the proxy for the reaction in the viewer.

**When to use:** During the research phase of any video. Applies to both entertainment content (novelty shock) and educational content (targeted shock against a viewer's specific pain point and prior belief).

**Source:** how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

### 4. Shock Score

**Definition:**

> "When you research a topic, you want to watch and read everything you can and then assess every fact you hear on a shock score of one to 100. Out of a 100 people, how many do you think would have heard that thing before or not? If your best guess is about four out of five people wouldn't have heard it, well then that's going to be a shock score of around 80 out of 100. The goal is to find facts that are true but also have a maximum shock score. So as you find them, take those facts and write them down as bullets on your script document."
>
> "This works for entertainment content like the Iron Man example, and it also works for all educational content. What do you know to be true about this topic that most people don't? Write those things down. That's where true interestingness lives."

— Source: how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

**How to apply:**

1. As you research, rate each fact from 1–100: out of 100 people in your target audience, how many would not have heard this?
2. Keep only the facts with high shock scores (70+).
3. Write those high-shock facts as bullet points on your script document — these become your body content and anchor your hook.
4. Aim for 5–10 high-shock bullets before writing anything else.

**When to use:** During the research/fact-gathering phase, before writing the hook or outline. The shock score is the filter that separates what goes in the script from what gets cut.

**Source:** how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

### 5. 360 Mapping

**Definition:**

The 360 Mapping process is listing all possible angles on a topic and scoring each one. Rather than taking the first angle that comes to mind, you generate the full landscape of possible approaches to the topic and select the one with the highest potential before committing to a direction.

> "The topic is the one sentence simple description of what the video is about. But the angle is the combination of the premise, take, and interesting key facts that are used to explain the topic. So, you could have two branding videos about Apple. One of them about the history of their logo design and the other one about a specific ad campaign from 2004. These are very different videos. Similar topic, completely different angles."

> "Now, your goal is to find common patterns across the winners. Maybe you notice that six out of the top 10 performing videos in your niche are all talking about the same topic. Or maybe when you zoom out and analyze the top 50 best performers, you keep seeing the same visual format that grabs your attention over and over. These patterns are indicating there is a proven answer for that Lego brick in your niche."

— Source: the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas; how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

**How to apply:**

1. Write the topic in one sentence.
2. List every angle you can think of: different premises, different takes, different entry points into the topic.
3. For each angle, estimate its shock score (how far is it from what the viewer already knows?) and its applicability (can the viewer do something with this?).
4. Cross-reference against outlier data: which angles have already performed well for this topic in your niche?
5. Select the angle that scores highest on both shock and applicability while having a gap in existing outlier coverage.

**When to use:** After you have your topic but before you commit to an angle. Most creators skip this step and take the first angle that comes to mind — which is usually the most obvious one, and therefore the least interesting.

**Source:** the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas, how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

### 6. White Space Analysis

**Definition:**

White Space Analysis is finding the gaps in existing outlier videos — the things they did not cover, did not explain clearly, or executed weakly — that you can upgrade in your version.

> "You look for gaps, identify where they were weak, and then try to make your version better than the weak points of the best videos."
>
> "Am I interested about this? What did they say? What were certain lines they said that piqued my interest? Were there visuals they use? Are there gaps that I'm seeing that could be potentially filled?"
>
> "Based on those answers, you're going to assess where the white space is. What was weak about that outlier that you could improve on to make an even better version."

— Source: the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

**How to apply:**

1. After watching an outlier video, ask: what did it do well? What did it not do well? What questions did it raise but not answer?
2. Log the gaps explicitly in your doc under the video's entry.
3. Look for gaps that cluster across multiple outliers — a gap that appears in five videos in a row is a confirmed white space in the market.
4. Your version's angle should close the most important gap while keeping what worked.

**When to use:** While doing outlier research. White space analysis converts watching videos passively into strategic intelligence.

**Source:** the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

### 7. Hold Constant Remix Rest

**Definition:**

> "Once you identify winning patterns in the idea Lego bricks, you want to hold the winning components constant and remix the rest. So, for example, let's say we make fitness content and we notice that all the best performing videos are hooking in the same way and using the same couple visual formats. We'll take those two components, keep them the same, but then we will change the topic, the angle, the story structure, the key visuals, the audio. So, in this case, it's hold two remix 5."
>
> "I like to think of this process kind of like playing five card poker. You see some reference outlier, you break it into seven cards. You pick which cards to keep and then you reshuffle the rest to get a new set of seven. This makes a new combination which has a higher chance of outperforming the original cuz you kept the good parts and you fix the bad."
>
> "Typically for me, what I've noticed is I will almost always change the angle, the hook, and the visual format. But usually, I will leave the topic, some of the key visuals, and usually like the audio mood of it. But this is all an iterative process. It changes topic to topic. It changes niche to niche."

— Source: the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

**How to apply:**

1. Analyze your outlier across all seven Lego bricks.
2. Identify which bricks drove the performance (usually topic, hook format, visual format).
3. Hold those 1–3 bricks constant in your version.
4. Remix the remaining bricks: new angle, new story structure, different key visuals, different audio — anything that closes the white space gaps you found.
5. The new combination has a higher hit probability than either all-original (too risky) or direct copy (no differentiation).

**When to use:** When you have identified a strong outlier and want to make your version. This is the execution step that converts outlier research into a concrete video concept.

**Source:** the-science-based-method-to-create-irresistible-ideas

### 8. Framework Remixing / 3% Rule

**Definition:**

> "Virgil Abloh was a famous designer that came up with the 3% rule. He said, 'All you have to do to make something your own is take something that already works, change it 3%, and then you've got something new.' What I just described is the 3% philosophy for content ideation. Except for you, you can remix 20, 30, even 50% still using that same content idea. And it will work because humans behave the same way over and over."
>
> "I promise you, if you approach your idea generation like this, your hit rate for winners, how often you have a viral video, will go way up. And this is the process that all the top creators, the top brands, and the top agencies are using to come up with content every single day that outperforms."

— Source: everything-you-know-about-content-is-probably-wrong

> "I tested 100 AI tools, these 9 will blow up your content" becomes "I studied 10,000 videos, these mistakes are killing your content." Same skeleton, different content. The sentence was written by a human, delivered by a human, and validated by millions of views.

— Derived pattern from PLAN.md

**How to apply:**

1. Find a video framework that has proven outlier performance.
2. Extract the skeleton: what is the structural promise? (e.g., "I studied X, here are Y things that Z")
3. Swap the domain, the number, the subject — but preserve the structural promise and delivery rhythm.
4. You can swap as little as 3% or as much as 50% and still benefit from the proven framework.
5. Do not treat this as cheating. This is how all media companies, top creators, and brands generate content that performs.

**When to use:** When you are stuck generating ideas. The 3% rule is a permission structure: you do not need to invent from scratch.

**Source:** everything-you-know-about-content-is-probably-wrong

### 9. Seven Story Structures

**Definition:**

> "There are seven winning story structures that tend to work across every niche. And just like hooks, I'm going to walk through all seven, give you the sauce, and tell you exactly how to use them."

The seven structures:

**Breakdown:**
> "The breakdown story structure is really used to explain any concept in building blocks. Essentially, you're breaking down some complex thing. The outline goes: hook, initial context shock, block explaining what happened or how it works, block for why it matters, outro. Best for: product launches, general development or news, new technology or analysis-based stories. Best hooks: secret reveal, question, comparison, contrarian."

**Newscaster:**
> "The newscaster story structure is used to explain a largely factual recounting of an event. Typically, not as much opinion, more facts. It's like a journalist. The outline: hook, full context of the story, optional extremely short take or analysis section, outro. Best for: journalism or sharing the news. Best hooks: secret reveal, question, comparison, contrarian, problem."

**Case Study Explainer:**
> "The case study explainer story structure is used whenever you're trying to educate a viewer on some blueprint or framework or process that was used to achieve some outcome in some way. The outline: hook, the framework (typically two to four pieces breaking down in succession), outro. Best when you're trying to teach skills or break down some framework quickly. Best hooks: case study hook, question hook, comparison hook."

**Listicle:**
> "The listical story structure is when you're trying to educate a viewer using an ordered list of items. The outline: hook, the list (three to five items), outro. Best used when you have a mutually exclusive list of atomic units, things that you can break apart in separation. Best hooks: list hook, problem hook."

**Problem Solver:**
> "The problem solver story structure is when you're trying to agitate a pain to tee up some solution. Problem, agitation, solution. The outline: hook, problem (agitate), solution, outro. Best when you're trying to lead with the problem. Best hooks: problem hook, question hook, contrarian hook."

**Tutorial:**
> "The tutorial story structure is really used as like a ride along or a recap of an experiment or some walkthrough of a system. The outline: hook, the tutorial (list-based step-by-step flow), outro. Best for education, explaining uncharted territory, breaking down a complex system. The difference between tutorial and list: for tutorial, you have a series of steps that all go in succession leading to one outcome. They can't live alone. For list, every unit can live on its own."

**Educational / Personal Story:**
> "This story structure is really used for storytelling from the first person perspective. The outline: hook, personal experience story (start, middle, end), outro. Used to illustrate first-person learnings. Typically leads to motivation, inspiration. Best hooks: personal experience hook, problem hook, contrarian hook."

— Source: how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

**How to apply:**

1. After selecting your angle and shock facts, pick one of the seven structures as your story skeleton.
2. Use outlier data to find which 1–2 structures work best in your niche for your topic type.
3. Match the structure to the type of value you are delivering: facts (newscaster), framework (case study), list (listicle), process (tutorial), pain point (problem solver), concept (breakdown), personal (educational).
4. Write the outline using the structure's blocks as section headers, then slot your high-shock facts into each block.

**When to use:** After you have your angle and facts, before writing the full script. The story structure is the container that holds everything together.

**Source:** how-to-script-viral-videos-10x-faster-nobody-teaches-this

### 10. Content Diagnostic

**Definition:**

> "There are three main problem areas that your video might be struggling with. Think of them like sections in a funnel. To get a viewer to watch and share, you need to get them through all three sections."

The three funnel sections:

**Click (top of funnel):**
> "The first and most important problem area to solve is the click. And specifically, the problem is that the click is not happening. There's two specific problems within this click section. The first is the matching problem — your videos are not being served to the right people. The second is the packaging problem — the algorithms are successfully putting your videos in front of the right people, but they're just not clicking."

**Opt-in (middle of funnel):**
> "The middle section is called the opt-in. And the problem here is that the viewer is not opting in to keep watching. There are four specific problems: the idea problem (your video idea just isn't valuable enough), the hook problem (your hook was not executed strongly enough), the comprehension problem (confusing language, complex words, passive voice), and the boredom problem (speed to value or speed of delivery is too slow)."

**Share (bottom of funnel):**
> "There's only two remaining problems standing between you and the share. The first is the takeaway problem — people share things because they believe they're going to get some form of social or professional credit. The final problem is the uniqueness problem — how unique is the final video as a complete package?"

> "Like any funnel, if your viewer gets stuck at the top, well, then nothing else below that point matters. So, your biggest priority is solving the problems in the top section first, then the middle section, and then the bottom."

— Source: why-your-content-isn-t-getting-views-and-how-to-fix-it

**How to apply:**

1. When a video underperforms, run the diagnostic top to bottom. Do not jump to editing fixes if the idea or matching is broken.
2. Click stage: Is the audience match correct? Is the title + thumbnail triggering pain + solve + proof?
3. Opt-in stage: Is the idea novel, applicable, and unique? Does the hook open a curiosity loop? Is the language simple and direct? Is the speed to value fast?
4. Share stage: Does the video have a clear tactical takeaway? Does it have a wow factor or signature element?
5. Fix issues in order. A brilliant hook cannot save a bad idea. A brilliant idea cannot save bad packaging.

**When to use:** As a pre-publish checklist for every video. Also use retroactively when diagnosing underperforming content.

**Source:** why-your-content-isn-t-getting-views-and-how-to-fix-it

## What He Does But Doesn't Teach

**His own videos use the Shock Score on the title level, not just in the body.** Every Kallaway title contains at least one high-shock claim: "I studied 10,000 videos," "these mistakes are killing your content," "nobody teaches this." The title itself is a scored fact — chosen because most viewers haven't seen it framed that way before.

**He applies 360 Mapping before naming videos.** Titles like "why the smartest creators I know focus on packaging, not content" are angles, not topics. The topic is packaging. The angle — smartest people I know + counterintuitive pivot from content to packaging — is the 360 result. He never explains this step explicitly, but the pattern appears across all his titles.

**He uses Hold Constant Remix Rest on his own series structure.** His videos consistently hold constant: practitioner credibility claim ("I have a million followers, I've done billions of views"), a numbered framework structure (3 steps, 7 types, 10 tips), and a personal story proof point. He remixes: the domain, the specific numbers, the hook format.

**He runs the Content Diagnostic implicitly in his intros.** His standard intro move is to name the pain point, state the viewer's current false belief, and immediately promise the correct framework. This is him confirming the click diagnostic check in real time — proving to the viewer at 0–10 seconds that the idea is novel, applicable, and relevant.

**He treats the 3% rule as a production habit, not an exception.** His titles are structural remixes of proven formats: "I studied X videos, these N things will Y" / "nobody teaches this" / "why [counterintuitive claim]." These structures recur across different topics because they are his validated hook and title skeleton — held constant while topics change.

**He uses white space analysis to pick his episode topics.** When he makes a video on hooks, storytelling, or packaging, he consistently positions it as filling a gap: "nobody gives away this kind of stuff for free," "this is the first time I covered ideas on this channel in a meaningful way." Whether or not that claim is precise, it reflects his selection process: he is picking topics where he perceives the existing coverage to be weak or superficial.

> "When you research a topic, you want to watch and read everything you can and then assess every fact you hear on a shock score of one to 100. Out of a 100 people, how many do you think would have heard that thing before or not? If your best guess is about four out of five people wouldn't have heard it, well then that's going to be a shock score of around 80 out of 100. The goal is to find facts that are true but also have a maximum shock score."

**What makes it work:** Operationalizes an abstract judgment (is this interesting?) into a concrete 1–100 number. Anyone can run this filter without special skill.

## Checklist

**Research and Fact Gathering**
- [ ] Identify topic in one sentence
- [ ] Watch and read all major sources on the topic
- [ ] Rate every fact encountered on a shock score (1–100)
- [ ] Write down all facts scoring 70+ as bullets
- [ ] Aim for 5–10 high-shock fact bullets before moving forward

**Outlier Research**
- [ ] Build or review watch list of top 20–30 channels in niche
- [ ] Filter by outlier score 5x+, engagement rate 5%+, relevant post date
- [ ] Sort by outlier score descending
- [ ] Watch each outlier video and log findings per Lego brick (topic, angle, hook format, story structure, visual format, key visuals, audio)
- [ ] Identify white space gaps in each outlier: what was weak or missing?
- [ ] Look for gaps that appear across multiple outliers (confirmed white space)

**360 Mapping**
- [ ] List every possible angle on the topic (minimum 5)
- [ ] Score each angle on shock (distance from viewer's prior belief) and applicability (can viewer act on it?)
- [ ] Cross-reference against outlier angles already covered
- [ ] Select the angle with highest combined score and lowest existing coverage

**Idea Assembly**
- [ ] Identify which 1–3 Lego bricks from top outliers to hold constant
- [ ] Apply Hold Constant Remix Rest: keep the winning bricks, remix the rest
- [ ] Apply the 3% Rule frame: confirm you are not reinventing from scratch unnecessarily
- [ ] Confirm the new combination closes at least one white space gap

**Story Structure Selection**
- [ ] Review which 1–2 story structures perform best in your niche for this topic type
- [ ] Select one story structure from the seven (breakdown, newscaster, case study explainer, listicle, problem solver, tutorial, educational/personal)
- [ ] Match story structure to the type of value being delivered

**Content Diagnostic Pre-Check (before scripting)**
- [ ] Click check: will this idea be served to the right audience? Does the title/thumbnail frame trigger pain + solve + proof?
- [ ] Opt-in check: is the idea novel, applicable, and unique? Is there a clear curiosity loop?
- [ ] Share check: does the concept have a clear tactical takeaway and a wow element?
- [ ] Write the desired emotion at the top of your script doc as a filter for all writing decisions
