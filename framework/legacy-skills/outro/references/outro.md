# Outro

> The outro is the final impression — the last 30–90 seconds that determine whether a viewer subscribes, shares, revisits, or disappears. Kallaway treats it as a precision tool, not an afterthought.

## Overview

The outro is phase 5 of Kallaway's 5-step script writing process. He flags it explicitly: "it feels like the outro would be an afterthought like who would be listening to this point but you can add retention and shares and likes and rewatches at a much higher clip if you just do this at the very end of the video."

The outro serves two mechanical functions:
1. **Close the psychological loop** — remind the viewer the pain they came in with is now solved (reality beat expectations)
2. **Convert attention to action** — native CTAs that extend the relationship without feeling like an ad

Six concepts govern how Kallaway builds outros. They are ordered here as they typically stack in practice: close the loop → recap → unexpected surprise → emotional payoff → last dab → native embed CTA.

## Concepts

### 1. Close the Loop

**Definition:** "At the end of the video tell the viewer that you have successfully closed the paino solution Gap and that by doing this they should have everything they need to be able to take the information in the video and solve for it themselves but if they would like to increase the speed and get to the solution faster then they can take your action or purchase your product"
— *18 Psychology Tricks to Get Viewers Addicted to Your Videos*

**How to apply:**
1. Explicitly state that the pain point introduced at the top of the video has now been addressed
2. Remind the viewer what they now have that they didn't have before
3. Create a soft gap: "you have the full map, but if you want speed, here's the shortcut" (this primes the CTA)
4. Do NOT close the loop with "that's it!" — close it by restating the value delivered

**When to use:** Every video. This is the psychological bookend to the pain you opened in the hook. If you twisted the knife in the intro, you must close the wound at the outro — otherwise the viewer leaves with unresolved tension and negative association.

**Source:** *18 Psychology Tricks to Get Viewers Addicted to Your Videos*

### 2. Recap

**Definition:** "remind them of the paino solve that you walked through... leave the viewer with a lasting impression of wow that was an amazing video I just remembered all these tactics that I learned the reality was much higher than my expectations"
— *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

"tell the viewer what you're going to do do it and then remind them at the end of what you did that reminding is the recap this is the final nail that closes the loop on that paino solution Gap"
— *18 Psychology Tricks to Get Viewers Addicted to Your Videos*

**How to apply:**
1. Number each point — say "the first was..., the second was..., the third was..."
2. One-line summary per point — compress each concept to its atomic form
3. Deliver the recap at roughly 2x the pace of the body (compression device)
4. Optional: add a meta-summary ("the biggest one is X") for the most impactful point
5. Keep it punchy — this is a confirmation of what they got, not a second teaching

**When to use:**
- Listicle and numbered-point videos (mandatory — the recap IS the outro for these)
- Any video with 3+ distinct concepts
- When the video is long (10+ minutes) and a viewer may have skimmed — recap gives them the condensed payoff

**Source:** *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*; *How to write a script so good viewers can't stop watching*; *18 Psychology Tricks to Get Viewers Addicted to Your Videos*; *6 Content Mistakes That Are KILLING Small Creators*; *It took me 1,000 hours to learn this about content*

### 3. Unexpected Surprise

**Definition:** "most of Life boils down to expectations verse reality if they expected a highquality masterpiece in your video but you fell short chances are they're not going to take the action but if the reality of watching the video blew away their expectations especially if it was because of how valuable the information actually was they are way more likely to take the action that you suggested"
— *18 Psychology Tricks to Get Viewers Addicted to Your Videos*

"leave them with a high note... you want to leave the viewer with a lasting impression of wow that was an amazing video"
— *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

"this is the free chocolate that the restaurant gives you when they bring the check they're doing that on purpose they're trying to remind you of the good service that they invested the whole previous two hours"
— *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

**How to apply:**
1. Deliver a bonus tip, insight, or reveal AFTER the main content is finished — something the viewer didn't expect to get
2. Frame it as bonus: "before we end this video I want to quickly share one bonus tip..." or "one more thing..."
3. The surprise must be genuinely useful — if it's padding, it reads as padding
4. This is optionally where a deep psychology insight or contrarian observation can land hardest — it's unexpected, so the contrast is maximum
5. Aim for 12 out of 10 quality on the surprise moment: "I try to make all of my videos at a 12 out of 10 quality that blows people away so this the unexpected surprise works every time"

**When to use:**
- When you have a bonus concept that didn't fit cleanly into the body structure
- When you want to reward viewers who stayed to the end (algorithm signal: completion)
- When introducing a CTA — the surprise creates emotional surplus that makes the CTA feel like a gift rather than a sell

**Source:** *18 Psychology Tricks to Get Viewers Addicted to Your Videos*; *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

### 4. Emotional Payoff

**Definition:** End on a feeling, not on information. The outro should leave the viewer in an emotional state — not just informed, but energized, validated, or hopeful. This is the mechanism behind algorithm-driving actions: likes, shares, comments, rewatches all come from an emotional state, not a cognitive one.

"this is where likes comments shares rewatches all the good stuff that powers the algorithm it all kind of comes from leaving them with a high note"
— *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

"Emotional Payoff — triggers macro viral share loops"
— *notes.md*

**How to apply:**
1. After the recap, shift from informational to relational tone: "I make these videos because I genuinely want [their goal]"
2. Show authentic investment in their success — not performative ("you could hear it in my voice I'm giving everything I have")
3. Connect the payoff back to the viewer's identity: "if you're a business owner trying to make better content this channel is for you"
4. Use optimism as the closing energy: "if you have that rigor you'll get whatever you want after 12 months" / "you will unlock anything you could possibly imagine"
5. Avoid ending on information — end on a human moment

**When to use:** Every video. The emotional payoff doesn't require a separate section — it can be woven into the recap and CTA. The key is that the final emotional register is warmth, confidence, or excitement rather than "that's all I've got."

**Source:** *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*; *How to Make Addictive Videos*; *6 Content Mistakes That Are KILLING Small Creators*; *notes.md*

### 5. Last Dab

**Definition:** "I like to call the last line of my script the last dab because I want it to be so memorable that if that's all someone heard they'd be willing to share it with a friend"
— *How To Become A Master Storyteller*

"in short form video especially the video is designed to Loop so the last line is actually a setup for the first lines as it replays I think of this a lot like baseball the nine hitter is supposed to set the table for the top of the order"
— *How To Become A Master Storyteller*

**How to apply:**
1. Write the last line FIRST when scripting — before the body, before the intro
2. Ask: if this was the ONLY line someone heard, would they repeat it or share it?
3. Test for loop quality: does the last line set up the first line if the video replays? (short form loop mechanic)
4. Make it quotable — short, punchy, portable: "the only way you lose is if you stop posting" / "if you strike gold it'll keep working"
5. Avoid generic sign-offs ("that's all I've got for this video") as the actual last line — push the emotional weight earlier, use the sign-off as the transition out, but keep the last dab BEFORE the sign-off

**Direction principle:** "the best place to start when you're writing a story is the end figure out what the end is going to be and then work backwards from there"
— *How To Become A Master Storyteller*

**When to use:** Every video — but especially:
- Short-form (loop mechanic is literal)
- Long-form authority videos where you want a quotable summary sentence
- Any video you want to be shared as a clip

**Source:** *How To Become A Master Storyteller*; *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

### 6. Native Embed CTA

**Definition:** "my strategy for this is what I call native embeds what you want to do when you're creating that outline is figure out proactively which point naturally suits highlighting the free resource you have as the solve and so when you're speaking about the point you want to frame the pain point and then give your own Resource as the solve it'll seem way smoother and more seamless if you do it this way"
— *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

**Structure — Layered CTA Funnel:**
> "Free video → Free community → Newsletter → Product"
— *notes.md*

**How to apply:**
1. **Identify the natural anchor point** — which concept in the body creates a pain-that-still-exists opening? Use that as the CTA setup
2. **Two mechanics to embed:**
   - Open the loop with a pain point, give your free resource as the solve
   - Speed-run a framework so fast most can't absorb it, then offer the free resource as a recap/compressed version
3. **Layer the funnel softly:** lead with the free resource (video, community, newsletter), not the product — trust is built in sequence
4. **Make it feel like teaching, not advertising:** "you may not have even picked up that I called those out" — the ideal native embed is invisible
5. **Multiple embeds per video are fine** if each is anchored to a genuine pain point: "I did this twice in this video"
6. **Outro CTA formula:** state the community/resource, state who it's for, state that it's free, give the action (link in description)

**Key rule:** "you don't want it to seem like you're breaking flow in the script you just want to seamlessly add it in showing it natively and then move on"
— *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*

**When to use:**
- Every video, at minimum once — but can appear mid-body as well as outro
- Use free community or newsletter CTA when video is educational/authority content
- Use product CTA only after building substantial trust (multiple free resources first)
- For short-form: single CTA, make it the lightest possible ask

**Source:** *How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked*; *18 Psychology Tricks to Get Viewers Addicted to Your Videos*

## What He Does But Doesn't Teach

Meta-analysis of Kallaway's own video endings — the outro techniques demonstrated in practice, not just taught.

### He always recaps but disguises it as a "summary"

In every major video, Kallaway runs a compressed ordinal recap before the final CTA. In *How To Write A Killer Script*, the recap runs: "Again this was a step-by-step formula... the full framework and that CTA conversion native embedded piece at the end this was a full Master Class." In *How to write a script so good viewers can't stop watching*, it reads: "Now as a summary, these were the three mistakes we went through and the tactical fixes... The first mistake... Now, the second mistake... And lastly, the third mistake we covered was..."

He never labels this as "the recap" — he says "as a summary" or "as always guys" or "to reorient us." The teaching name and the practical execution use different vocabulary.

### He closes his own pain loop in the outro

In *6 Content Mistakes That Are KILLING Small Creators*, the hook creates pain around "why your content is being ignored." The outro directly closes that loop: "If you feel like your content is being ignored, you're getting low views. If you're getting low views, it's because the sample data is coming back weak..." He traces the entire causal chain back to the original pain. This isn't incidental — it's the close the loop technique in action.

### The sign-off formula is consistent and structural

Almost every video ends with the same sign-off pattern:
1. Gratitude/validation line ("as always guys I'm trying to bring the stuff most people don't talk about")
2. Community or resource CTA ("if you haven't joined Wavy World there's no reason you shouldn't be in there")
3. Comment invite ("leave a comment and let me know what you want me to cover in the next video")
4. Sign-off phrase ("we will see you guys on the next one")
5. Closer ("peace")

This formula is so consistent it functions as a format fingerprint — viewers know the video is ending when they hear the cadence begin.

### He embeds authentic vulnerability into the emotional payoff

In *6 Content Mistakes*, he says: "I had a doubt this week that I was considering never making videos again on this channel just cuz I was so burnt out. And I mean that sincerely." This is emotional payoff via authentic admission — not a scripted inspirational line but a real personal moment. It creates connection and makes the subsequent CTA feel earned rather than mechanical. He does this periodically rather than every video, which preserves the impact.

### The CTA always leads with community (lowest friction) before product (highest friction)

Across all videos examined, the CTA funnel is consistently ordered: free community (Wavy World) → free newsletter (Content Department) → paid product (Sandcastles, programs). He never leads with a paid ask. The free community CTA ("it's completely free, invite link in the description") is the overwhelmingly dominant CTA type. Paid product CTAs (Sandcastles, short-form.academy) are embedded mid-body rather than reserved for the outro — a deliberate positioning choice that makes the outro feel less commercial.

### The "last dab" is often emotional, not tactical

His actual closing lines tend to be relational rather than informational. Examples:
- "please leave me a comment and let me know that I should keep going" (*6 Content Mistakes*)
- "keep me posted on what you thought of this video" (*Master Storyteller*)
- "we will see you guys on the next one peace" (every video)

The last dab in Kallaway's outros is rarely a quotable tactic — it's a relational anchor. The quotable tactical lines appear in the body (where sharing happens), while the outro closes the emotional cycle.

## Template Bank

### Outro — Close the Loop + Recap (Listicle)

**Original (source: *How to write a script so good viewers can't stop watching*):**
> "All right, guys, that's all I've got for this video. We covered the three really biggest script writing and storytelling mistakes that are probably holding back your content from performing better. Now, as a summary, these were the three mistakes we went through and the tactical fixes for how to solve each one. The first mistake or problem was the story flow problem... The way to solve this is to audit every line of your script after you've written it... Now, the second mistake, which is a huge one, is the comprehension problem... And lastly, the third mistake we covered was the speedtovalue problem... To solve for this, you want to follow the rough story outlined approach... Outro, summarize or extend the value."

**Template:**
> "All right, [informal closer]. We covered the [SWAP: number] really biggest [SWAP: topic] mistakes that are probably holding back your [SWAP: result] from performing better. Now, as a summary, these were the [SWAP: number] [SWAP: mistakes/concepts/steps] we went through and the [SWAP: tactical fixes/key moves/principles] for [SWAP: outcome]. The first [SWAP: mistake/concept] was [SWAP: name]. [One-line definition]. The way to solve this is [SWAP: core fix in one sentence]. Now, the second [SWAP: mistake/concept]... And lastly, the [SWAP: ordinal] [SWAP: mistake/concept] we covered was [SWAP: name]... [Core fix]."

**What makes it work:** The ordinal recap forces compression — one sentence per concept. Viewers who skimmed to the end still get the full value map. Each point named again reinforces memory consolidation.

## Checklist

- [ ] Close the loop: state explicitly that the pain from the hook has been addressed
- [ ] If listicle or numbered-point format: run a compressed ordinal recap (one line per point)
- [ ] Include at least one unexpected surprise or bonus element (a concept, insight, or resource not previewed)
- [ ] End on a feeling, not on information — last register should be warmth, optimism, or genuine investment
- [ ] Write the last dab first: test whether the final line is memorable enough to stand alone and be shared
- [ ] For short-form: check that the last line sets up the first line if the video loops
- [ ] Native embed CTA: anchor to a real pain point raised in the body, not bolted on at the end
- [ ] CTA funnel order: free resource (video, community, newsletter) before paid product
- [ ] If using a community or newsletter CTA: state explicitly that it's free and describe who it's for
- [ ] Comment invite: ask a specific question (what do you want to see next / was this helpful) not a generic "leave a comment"
- [ ] Sign-off: use the consistent formula — "we will see you on the next one" or equivalent
- [ ] Audit tone: does the outro feel like the restaurant's free chocolate or an ad break?
