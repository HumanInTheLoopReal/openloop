# Value Loop: Complete Guide

> How to structure each body point so viewers stay, understand, and act — including the macro architecture that governs everything between hook and outro.

## Overview

The body is not one undifferentiated block of content. It has architecture. At the macro level, it alternates body segments with rehooks in a repeating rhythm. At the micro level, every individual teaching point follows a three-step loop that matches how the brain processes information. Miss either layer and you lose viewers — even if your ideas are solid.

This guide covers two scales of body architecture. The micro scale is the Value Loop (Context → Application → Framing) — the internal structure of every point. The macro scale is the Cohesive Body Architecture — the sequential blueprint of the whole script from first body segment through outro. Both come from Kallaway's scripting tutorials.

Additional concepts in this guide: Point Sequencing (which order to put your points in), the Outro sequence (how to close the loop and maximize action), Click Confirmation (the critical bridge between title promise and first body point), Bad Value Loop anti-patterns (what failure looks like), and Shock Value / Shock Score (how to select which points are worth building around).

## Value Loop (Context → Application → Framing)

### What It Is

For each point in the body you want to include these three things — Kallaway calls it the value loop:

1. **Context** — say what it is and explain it as simple as possible
2. **Application** — say how to do it and use as many relevant examples to kind of articulate your point as possible
3. **Framing** — say why this point matters and how it fits into the overall puzzle of the full story

The context part needs to be clear, concise, and digestible. The application has to be interesting enough and easy to digest where they can begin taking action on their own side. And the framing needs to reorient them so when they zoom out they can see why it's worth continuing to watch the video.

This three-step frame suits the way the brain processes information: base facts first, surface level, then slowly deepening to add inference and connect different points.

### How to Apply

For every body point you write, run through the three legs before moving on:

1. **Context (WHAT):** Can you state the concept in one sentence? If you can't say it simply, you don't understand it well enough yet.
2. **Application (HOW):** Give at least one concrete example. Kallaway's instruction is to use "as many relevant examples as possible" — examples are not optional decoration, they are the application.
3. **Framing (WHY):** End the point by zooming out. Answer: "Why does this matter relative to the larger goal of the video?" If you skip framing, the viewer loses the thread.

After writing all three, ask: does skipping any one of them leave the viewer unclear about what it is, unable to act on it, or unsure why they need it? If yes, the loop is incomplete.

### When to Use

Every single body point, every format, every video type. There are no exceptions. Whether you are writing a numbered list, a case study narrative, a how-to, or an opinion piece — each discrete teaching moment requires all three legs. The loop is the minimum viable unit of instruction.

### What He Does But Doesn't Teach

Kallaway teaches the three-part loop as a prescription, but his own application reveals a fourth implicit element: **escalation framing**. The framing leg rarely just says "this matters." It almost always hints that the NEXT thing matters even more. In practice, the "why this fits into the bigger puzzle" becomes setup for the next point — turning the close of one loop into the opening of the next.

## Cohesive Body Architecture (Hook → Body → Rehook → Body → Outro)

### What It Is

This is the macro-level blueprint for the whole script. Kallaway names it explicitly in his scripting tutorials:

> After the hook, the next section is the body. We'll call this the first body. And once you establish the context and the incoming value, you want to follow that story flow process... Okay, so we've got hook and then we have the first body. Now we have the rehook. And this happens about 20 to 25 seconds into a short form video and maybe 2 to 3 minutes into a YouTube video. A rehook is the reagravation of the painoint originally triggered in the hook by showing that there's a better solution still out there... So, so far we have the hook, we have the first body, and we have the rehook. The next section is the second body... And you repeat this back and forth flow. Rehook body. Rehook body. As long as you have valuable context to say. For a YouTube video, you might rehook four or five times.

The sequenced blueprint:

```
Hook (value in first 3 seconds)
→ First Body (clear story flow, distilled details)
→ Rehook (~2-3 min in on YouTube, ~20-25s on short-form)
→ Second Body
→ Rehook
→ [repeat body → rehook until done]
→ Outro (summarize OR extend the value ramp)
```

The body is not a monolith. It alternates — body segment, rehook, body segment, rehook — for as many cycles as the video has valuable content to deliver.

### How to Apply

1. Write your outline as individual body segments, not one continuous body block.
2. After every 1-3 teaching points (depending on length), plan a rehook before the next segment begins.
3. Mark the intended cadence: on YouTube, a rehook approximately every 2-3 minutes. On short-form, approximately every 20-25 seconds.
4. At the end of every body segment, plan the transition line that will serve as the rehook — see `02-reh-hooking.md` for mechanics.
5. Write the outro as either (a) a summary of stated value, or (b) an extension of the value ramp (a final insight that makes the body feel even more important in retrospect).

### When to Use

Every YouTube video. This is the base architecture — not a technique to use sometimes, but the framework that contains all other techniques. Every other concept in this guide operates inside this structure.

### What He Does But Doesn't Teach

Kallaway describes this as a "rough schematic" with "a ton of nuance inside there." What he doesn't name explicitly: the body is a **speed-to-value architecture**. Every structural choice — where the rehooks land, how many body segments exist, how long each runs — is ultimately a decision about how quickly you deliver the next hit of value. The listener's attention decay curve is the forcing function. The blueprint is his answer to that curve.

## Point Sequencing (Second Best First)

### What It Is

Kallaway's cheat code for ordering body points:

> My cheat code for this which is not intuitive is to put your second best body point in that first slot... human brains work in patterns if you show someone one thing that's amazing they'll think it's cool but if you show them two things that are amazing especially if the second thing is better than the first it creates a subconscious pattern where they have to stay to see the third because they feel this value continuing to drip up over time if you show your best thing first and then your second best thing is slightly worse it trains the viewer subconsciously that the value will be diminishing... if you slightly increase from 1 to two it creates this pattern and they have to stay.

He also frames it as the album strategy:

> Your haymaker should go second on the list, intro, two, and then one. This is so that when the viewer consumes the first two points in your video, it feels like the value is going up from second best to best instead of down from best to second best.

The principle: **ascending value** creates subconscious anticipation momentum. Descending value creates subconscious permission to leave.

### How to Apply

1. Write out all your body points and rank them honestly by value/impact.
2. Take your #2 point and place it first.
3. Place your #1 point (the haymaker) in position 2 or 3.
4. Remaining points can fill out the rest in any logical order, but the ascending signal set by points 1-2 is the important move.
5. Test the feeling: does reading point 1 make you want to hear point 2? Does reading point 2 make the next point feel mandatory? If not, re-rank.

### When to Use

Any video with multiple discrete body points — especially numbered formats, lesson-extraction videos, and how-to structures. Less relevant for narrative-driven single-story videos where the sequence follows story logic rather than value ranking.

### What He Does But Doesn't Teach

Across his numbered videos, the highest-impact item rarely leads. He explains this principle in his scripting tutorial but doesn't flag it as format-specific advice. The implication is that it applies to any body point ordering — not just listicles. His own videos consistently demonstrate the ascending value pattern even when the content is not structured as an explicit numbered list.

## Click Confirmation + Intro Hook Framework

### What It Is

Click confirmation is the principle that the first few lines of the intro must confirm — and ideally beat — the expectations set by the title:

> Your job when you're writing the script is to make sure the first few lines of the intro confirm and ideally beat their expectations set by the title. This is called click confirmation... to exceed expectations he would also add on something like "I have unprecedented access to explore pieces that have never been explored before" — and this takes base expectations (the pyramids) and then beats them (unprecedented access). This positive gap of reality beating expectations hooks the viewer.

The 5-Part Intro Hook Framework that follows click confirmation:

1. **Immediate context** — what the video is about (viewer can opt in and confirm the click)
2. **Common belief** — establish the conventional take on the topic (viewer gets eager when you talk about something they care about)
3. **Contrarian take** — subvert the common belief with your actual approach
4. **Proof** — why they should trust your contrarian approach
5. **Plan** — an ordered list or steps for how you're going to do it

> By the end of this intro framework and I'm telling you it works for every video you have the viewer Hook Line and Sinker.

### How to Apply

1. Read your title back. What promise does it make?
2. Write your first line to directly confirm that promise — name what the video is about in the first 2-3 sentences.
3. Then go one level further: add an unexpected detail, scope, or angle that makes the title's promise feel like it underdelivered.
4. Work through the 5-part framework in sequence: common belief → contrarian take → proof → plan.
5. Test: can someone read the intro and know exactly what they're getting, why it's different from what they already know, and why you're the one to deliver it? If not, one of the five parts is weak.

### When to Use

Every video. Click confirmation is the bridge between thumbnail/title and the first body point. Without it, the body inherits confused or disconnected viewers. The 5-part framework applies most explicitly to educational/explainer formats and how-to content. For narrative/entertainment content, parts 4 and 5 (proof and plan) may be implicit rather than stated.

## Outro — Close the Loop / Unexpected Surprise / Recap

### What It Is

Three psychology hacks that form a complete ending sequence (from Kallaway's 18 psychology tricks):

**Hack #16 — Close the Loop:**
> Tell the viewer that you have successfully closed the pain/solution gap and that by doing this they should have everything they need to be able to take the information in the video and solve for it themselves. But if they would like to increase the speed and get to the solution faster then they can take your action or purchase your product... The hack is that you reminded them that the loop is closed because by reminding them it re-signals that pain point that they wanted to solve. They will feel like it's still unsolved because they haven't absorbed all the information and they will look to take an action to solve it.

**Hack #17 — Unexpected Surprise:**
> Most of life boils down to expectations versus reality. If they expected a high-quality masterpiece in your video but you fell short, chances are they're not going to take the action. But if the reality of watching the video blew away their expectations... they are way more likely to take the action that you suggested. I try to make all of my videos at a 12 out of 10 quality that blows people away.

**Hack #18 — Recap:**
> Tell the viewer what you're going to do, do it, and then remind them at the end of what you did. That reminding is the recap. This is the final nail that closes the loop on that pain/solution gap.

### How to Apply

1. Before writing the outro, check: have you closed the main pain/problem the video opened? State that explicitly — "You now have everything you need to [solve X]."
2. Add the unexpected surprise as a production standard, not a one-off: aim for the video to exceed the promise of the title. If the title is good, the content should be better.
3. End with a compressed recap of all points covered. Not a detailed re-explanation — a tight ordinal sweep ("first, second, third") that reminds the viewer of the full value they consumed.
4. Layer in your CTA after the loop-close: "But if you want to [get there faster / go deeper], [CTA]."

### When to Use

Every video. These three hacks form a single combined sequence, not options to choose between. The order matters: close the loop → (the unexpected surprise is a production standard throughout, not a single line) → recap → CTA.

## Bad Value Loop Examples (Anti-Patterns)

### What It Is

Two distinct failure modes that both produce a broken body, identified by Kallaway from different angles:

**Anti-pattern 1 — The Reporter Level (wrong sequence):**
> Level one storytelling is the foundation level. I call this level the reporter. The reason level one storytelling is so unengaging is because essentially all reporters do is read facts in order. This happened, then this happened, then that happened. They're just reporting what happened. When you tell stories in level one you collect all the Lego bricks — the characters, the mission, the scene — but the problem is you just lay them out for a viewer in a predictable, boring order.

The Reporter has the right facts but no contrast, no conflict, no escalation. The information is correct but inert.

**Anti-pattern 2 — The Prince in the Forest (unnecessary tangents):**
> Imagine that I was telling you a story that went like this. One day there was a prince walking through the forest... You were on a clear path. The prince walking to a house in the forest with animals. Clear path. But then I veered you off that path to throw in all these unnecessary details about the prince's background and loving animals and working at the stables. These are unnecessary details that didn't need to be there based on the purpose of this plot. And what this does in practice is that it caused your brain to hop storylines.

The Prince story has the right direction but adds unnecessary context that breaks the hypnosis. The viewer's brain leaves the story to process the tangent.

### How to Apply

The diagnostic question for every line in your body:

> "Is this a necessary piece of context or an intentional step in the logic flow? Or is it a distraction?"

If a line can be removed without breaking the viewer's understanding or the story's through-line, remove it. The Reporter test: does this body read like a sequence of facts with no tension? The Prince test: does this body have a clear destination the viewer is walking toward, or have you sent them off the path?

### When to Use

Use as an audit after drafting any body section. Before considering a script final, run both tests. The Reporter test reveals missing conflict/contrast. The Prince test reveals unnecessary detail inflation.

## Shock Value / Shock Score (Point Selection)

### What It Is

Interestingness is calculable. Kallaway uses a 1-100 shock score to evaluate which facts or claims are worth building a body point around:

> Interestingness comes from shock value. Shock value is the distance between what someone already knows about a topic and what you tell them in the video. The greater this distance, the greater the shock and the more they will hold on to see why that shocking claim might be true.

The scoring method:

> When you research a topic, you want to watch and read everything you can and then assess every fact you hear on a shock score of one to 100. Out of 100 people, how many do you think would have heard that thing before or not? If your best guess is about four out of five people wouldn't have heard it, well then that's going to be a shock score of around 80 out of 100. The goal is to find facts that are true but also have a maximum shock score.

The shock score is not about exaggeration or clickbait — it is the literal distance between the viewer's prior knowledge and the claim. High distance = high shock = high retention.

### How to Apply

1. During research, list every significant fact, claim, or perspective you encounter.
2. Score each one 1-100: "How many out of 100 people would already know this?"
3. Only build body points around the highest-scoring facts (score 60+). If a fact has a shock score below 40, your audience probably already knows it — it won't hold attention.
4. A shock score of 80+ is a body point anchor. These are the facts that make viewers pause, replay, or share.
5. Order your body points using the point sequencing principle — put #2 shock-value point first, #1 shock-value point second.

### When to Use

Research and outlining phase, before writing. Use this to decide which facts make it into the body at all, and which facts support the high-score claims as Application-leg examples.

## Checklist

Before locking a body section, verify:

- [ ] Every body point has all three legs: Context (WHAT), Application (HOW), Framing (WHY)
- [ ] Body points are ordered with second-best first, best second
- [ ] Body segments alternate with rehooks at the right cadence (every 2-3 min on YouTube)
- [ ] The intro opens with click confirmation — the first line names what the video delivers
- [ ] The intro hits all 5 framework steps: immediate context, common belief, contrarian take, proof, plan
- [ ] Every line in the body passes the audit: "necessary context, or distraction?"
- [ ] No Reporter-level sections: every stretch has contrast, conflict, or escalation
- [ ] No Prince-in-Forest detours: the viewer has a clear destination at all times
- [ ] Body points are anchored to high shock-score facts (60+ out of 100)
- [ ] Outro closes the loop explicitly, recaps what was covered, and positions the CTA after the loop-close

### Intro framework — 5-part sequence

**Original (source: how-to-write-a-killer-script-that-keeps-viewers-hooked):**
> okay so part one is to give immediate context on what the video is about... part two is to establish the common take or belief... the next part of the intro which is critical is building a contrarian take... you have to do two things: one, establish proof for why they should trust your new approach and two, give them a plan... by the end of this intro framework and I'm telling you it works for every video you have the viewer Hook Line and Sinker

**Template:**
> (1) Immediate context — what the video is about. (2) Common belief — the conventional take. (3) Contrarian take — why that belief is wrong or incomplete. (4) Proof — why trust your contrarian position. (5) Plan — your ordered steps for the video.

**What makes it work:** The sequence moves the viewer from "I've clicked" to "I need to stay" in five logical steps. Contrarian take creates tension. Proof releases it enough to trust. Plan delivers the roadmap.
