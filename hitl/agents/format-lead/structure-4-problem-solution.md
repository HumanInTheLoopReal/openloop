## Structure 4: The Problem-Solution Diagnostic

### What It Is

Three to five specific problems are identified, diagnosed, and solved. Unlike the listicle (which just names mistakes), the diagnostic goes deep on root causes. Unlike the framework (which installs a mental model), the diagnostic solves concrete problems. The viewer comes in with a specific pain ("my retention is dying") and leaves with a diagnosis and fix. Typically 3 problems because going deeper > going wider.

### Beat-by-Beat Flow

1. Hook: Specific symptom ("your content isn't getting views," "your hooks aren't working") + implied diagnosis promise
2. Click-confirm: Credibility + stakes (how much this costs you)
3. Diagnostic frame: Name the lens through which problems will be diagnosed (funnel, checklist, audit)
4. Problem 1: Name it → root cause analysis → tactical fix → example of what "fixed" looks like
5. Problem 2–N: Same structure; problems often ordered by where they appear in viewer journey
6. Synthesis: Architectural overview of how fixes connect (hook → body → rehook → outro pattern)
7. Outro: Full diagnostic checklist recap → priority order for fixing → CTA to linked deeper videos

### When to Use

When viewers have a specific pain they know they have. Best for topics with identifiable failure modes that have known fixes. The diagnostic framing ("find out why") beats pure advice ("here's what to do") because it positions the viewer as the subject of the analysis.

### Variations

- **Funnel-mapped variant:** Problems organized by position in the viewer journey or content funnel (top/middle/bottom funnel). Seen in "Why Your Content Isn't Getting Views." Adds architectural clarity.
- **Bucket variant:** Problems grouped into 3-4 categories rather than enumerated (ideas / storytelling / hooks / editing in "Give Me 18 Minutes"). Useful when there are too many individual problems to list.
- **Misconception-correction variant:** The "problem" is a false belief rather than a behavioral mistake. Seen in "Everything You Know Is Wrong," "Social Media Isn't Hard." Softer framing — "you're misunderstanding" rather than "you're failing."

### Signature Moves (Advanced Techniques)

- **The funnel architecture as diagnostic scaffold.** In "Why Your Content Isn't Getting Views," map problems to funnel position (click-through, retention, sharing). This gives the diagnostic architectural clarity — the viewer sees where in the system each problem lives.
- **The "four buckets" grouping move.** Instead of listing 12 individual mistakes, group them into 3-4 categories (ideas, storytelling, hooks, editing). This compression technique makes a large problem space feel manageable.
- **The "content diagnostic" as a reusable audit.** Frame the diagnostic as something the viewer can run on their own work. Naming the audit makes the methodology feel reusable and transferable.

#### Diagnostic problem — First hook mistake, delay
**Original (source: Give me 15 mins, and I'll make your hooks impossible to skip):**
> All right, the first hook mistake is called delay. And the good news is this one is the easiest one to fix. When I say delay, what I mean is that the context on what the video is going to be about is delayed too long in the video. If you were to visualize a script, let's say it has 13 lines and the third line is the first time you introduce the topic. This is delay because those first two lines are fluff and unnecessary details. You need to cut those first two lines, move the third line up to the top so your topic introduction can happen in the first 1 to 2 seconds. I often refer to this concept as speed to value. You want to increase the speed that the viewer can get to clarity or get to value. Here's an example of what bad delay looks like. Let's say you start your video with something like this. "Guys, this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen. And when you see it, you're never going to believe it." That is an example of a legit hook that many people try to use when they're beginners. Now, in theory, this kind of sounds like it might be a good hook because it's building suspense and curiosity about what that thing is. But the problem with that sentence is that it gives me as the viewer zero context on what is coming next. Every second you go without telling somebody what the video is about, so they have the information to decide to opt in, a large portion of your viewers are bouncing.

**Template:**
> All right, the first [SWAP: mistake/problem/trap] is called [SWAP: branded name]. And the good news is this one is the easiest one to fix. When I say [SWAP: branded name], what I mean is that [SWAP: plain-language definition]. If you were to visualize [SWAP: the thing], [SWAP: concrete visualization example]. This is [SWAP: the problem name] because [SWAP: why this is wrong]. You need to [SWAP: specific tactical fix]. I often refer to this concept as [SWAP: related term you're cross-referencing]. Here's an example of what [SWAP: bad version] looks like. [SWAP: Real example of the bad behavior]. That is an example of [SWAP: the mistake being exemplified]. The problem with this is [SWAP: why it fails]. Every [SWAP: time unit] you [SWAP: do the bad behavior], [SWAP: consequence for the audience].

**What makes it work:** The "visualize a script with 13 lines" makes an abstract concept concrete by giving it a specific physical form. The bad example ("Guys, this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen") is universally recognizable — every viewer has seen this type of hook, which makes the diagnosis stick.
